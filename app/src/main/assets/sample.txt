The Quiet Power of Reading

Most people who learn a foreign language eventually reach a frustrating plateau. They have memorized thousands of words from lists, yet when they open a real novel or a newspaper, the text still feels dense and impenetrable. The problem is rarely a lack of effort. It is that words learned in isolation tend to evaporate, because nothing in daily life ever summons them again.

Researchers who study language acquisition have a blunt explanation for this. A word is not truly acquired until a learner has encountered it many times, in varied contexts, and has noticed how it behaves next to other words. A flashcard can introduce a word, but it cannot show you that "reluctant" is usually followed by "to", or that people "harbour" suspicions but rarely "harbour" happiness.

Extensive reading offers a remedy. When you read a great deal of material that is slightly above your current level, unfamiliar words appear surrounded by familiar ones. The context does much of the heavy lifting: you can often infer a meaning before you ever consult a dictionary. Each later encounter quietly reinforces the memory, and the word gradually migrates from something you recognize to something you can actually use.

There is, however, a delicate balance to strike. If too many words on a page are unknown, comprehension collapses and reading becomes a tedious exercise in decoding. Studies suggest that learners read comfortably when roughly ninety-five to ninety-eight percent of the running words are already familiar. Below that threshold, the experience is exhausting; above it, there is little left to learn.

That is why choosing the right material matters so much. A good text should be compelling enough that you want to keep going, and challenging enough that you meet a handful of new words on every page. Fiction works well because stories create momentum, and momentum is what carries a reader through the inevitable moments of confusion.

Spaced repetition still has a role to play. The difference is that review no longer has to be a separate, joyless chore. When a word you are learning reappears in a book, that encounter is itself a review. Only the words that stubbornly refuse to come back on their own need a gentle nudge, ideally in the very sentence where you first met them.

In the end, vocabulary grows the way a garden does: not through a single dramatic effort, but through small, consistent attention over a long period of time. Read a little every day, notice the words that puzzle you, and let the stories do the rest.
