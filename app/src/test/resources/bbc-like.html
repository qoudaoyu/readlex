Why river towns are planting forests upstream

17 November 2026
ShareSave
Alex Rivers
Environment correspondent

For the second time in three years, shopkeepers in a small river town spent the new year pumping water out of their cellars.

The town council has now agreed to try something different: instead of building higher walls along the river, it will pay farmers further up the valley to plant trees.

Trees slow the water down. Their roots open the soil so that rain soaks in rather than running straight off the hills, and fallen branches create small dams that hold water back for hours.

A cheaper defence?

Supporters say the approach costs a fraction of a concrete wall. Critics reply that a forest takes decades to grow, while the next flood could arrive this winter.

The plan has three parts:

  • planting native trees on steep fields,
  • restoring old wetlands near the river,
  • and paying farmers for any land they give up.

"We can't stop the rain," said one farmer. "But we can decide how fast it reaches the town."

Similar schemes in other countries have reduced peak flood levels by around a fifth, according to researchers, though results depend heavily on the shape of the valley.
