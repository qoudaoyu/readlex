Cities Turn to Rooftop Farms as Summers Grow Hotter

By Jane Doe

On a flat roof six storeys above a busy junction, rows of tomatoes and beans are bending in the afternoon heat. The farm, which opened two years ago, now supplies three local restaurants and a school canteen, and its managers say demand is growing faster than they can plant.

City officials argue that projects like this do more than produce food. Plants and soil absorb sunlight that would otherwise heat bare concrete, and early measurements suggest that the rooms directly beneath the farm stay noticeably cooler on the hottest days of the year.

Not everyone is convinced. Engineers point out that many older buildings were never designed to carry the weight of wet soil, and strengthening a roof can cost more than the farm will ever earn. Insurance companies, too, remain cautious about water leaking into apartments below.

Supporters respond that the benefits are hard to measure in money alone. Volunteers who work on the roof describe it as a place to meet neighbours, and teachers bring pupils up to learn where vegetables come from, something many children in the area have never seen.

The city now plans to offer small grants to building owners who want to try. "We are not expecting every roof to become a farm," said one council member. "But if a few hundred do, the effect on the neighbourhood could be significant, especially as summers continue to get hotter."
